# Methods review: Robinson (1933), price discrimination

- **Paper:** Joan Robinson, *The Economics of Imperfect Competition*, London, Macmillan, 1933. The review covers
  Book V, chapter 15 ("Price Discrimination," pp. 179–202) and chapter 16 ("The Moral of Price Discrimination,"
  pp. 203–208), with the two formulas from Book I, chapter 2 (p. 36) and Book II, chapter 3 (p. 64) on which they
  rest.
- **Statistical focus:** the rule that a discriminating monopolist equates marginal revenue across separated markets
  and so charges the highest price where demand is least elastic (pp. 181–187), and the output comparison between
  simple and discriminating monopoly, including the straight-line equality result and the "adjusted concavity"
  condition (pp. 188–202).
- **Review standard:** internal consistency of the formal results, explicitness of assumptions, and the testability
  of predictions, as for any theoretical work; data-reporting standards (sample construction, uncertainty) are noted
  only to say they do not apply.
- **What was read:** the full text of the 1933 first edition, Foreword, Introduction, chapters 2, 3, 15 and 16, in
  the optical-character-recognition text of the Digital Library of India scan held by the Internet Archive
  (identifier in.ernet.dli.2015.217918). Several formulas in footnotes on pp. 193–194 are garbled in that text and
  were read from their surrounding prose. Pigou's *Economics of Welfare*, Yntema (1928) and Viner's *Dumping*,
  which Robinson discusses, were not examined. Later work on the welfare effects of third-degree discrimination
  (Schmalensee 1981; Varian 1985) was not read for this review.

---

## Methods summary

Chapter 15 is deductive geometry with mathematical footnotes. Robinson takes a single seller of one commodity who
can divide buyers into markets between which resale and transfer are impossible, with each market's demand
independent of prices in the others (p. 181). Using the relation between average and marginal curves from chapter
2, marginal revenue equals price × (e − 1)/e, she shows by lateral summation of the markets' marginal revenue curves
that profit is maximized where marginal revenue is the same in every market and equal to the marginal cost of total
output (pp. 181–183). Prices are therefore ranked inversely to the elasticities at the chosen prices (p. 187). She
then analyses a protected home market paired with a perfectly competitive export market (pp. 184–185), how a seller
free to choose the division of its market would split buyers (pp. 185–187), and the comparison of total output under
simple and discriminating monopoly (pp. 188–202). Chapter 16 draws welfare conclusions: discrimination may make
an industry viable that could not exist under one price, it helps the buyers whose price falls and hurts those
whose price rises, and its social desirability turns mainly on whether it raises output (pp. 203–206). It closes with
a proposal for regulated discriminating prices to maximize output under falling costs (pp. 207–208).

## Research design

Theoretical, with no empirical component. The design is a comparative-statics comparison of two regimes (one price
versus separate prices) for a profit-maximizing seller with given demand and cost curves. Assumptions are stated in
the text: profit maximization throughout (p. 206), independent demands across markets (p. 181), no resale (p. 179),
and the cost of administering discrimination ignored (p. 189, note).

## Sampling strategy

Does not apply. No data are collected. Illustrations (surgeons' fees, railway rates for cotton and coal, dumping,
branded goods, the village barber, the country doctor) are examples, not a sample.

## Sample size

Does not apply.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The analysis excludes cases where demand in one market depends on the price in another, such as Edgeworth's first-
and third-class railway fares (p. 181). It excludes competitive sellers on the ground that discrimination cannot
persist under perfect competition (p. 179). The output comparison treats two markets and generalizes to many in a
footnote (p. 194).

## Independent and dependent variables

In the comparative statics, the regime (single price or discrimination) is the exogenous change, and the outcomes
are the price in each market, output in each market, total output and profit. The demand elasticity in each market
at the simple-monopoly price, and the curvature of each demand curve, are the conditioning variables that determine
the sign of the output change.

## Statistical methods

None in the statistical sense. The methods are plane geometry of average and marginal curves, lateral summation, and
a local approximation for the output result that assumes the two markets' elasticities are not very different, so
that short arcs of the marginal revenue curves can be treated as straight lines (p. 193, note). Robinson credits
R. F. Kahn with the generalization of that approximation and M. H. A. Newman with analysis behind the proof
(pp. 190, 194).

## Reported effect sizes

No empirical effect sizes. The quantitative content is in the formulas and limiting cases listed in the extraction
table, for example that marginal revenue is half of price when elasticity is 2 (p. 36), and that with constant
marginal cost perfectly discriminating output is more or less than twice simple monopoly output as the demand curve
is concave or convex (p. 188, note).

## Confidence intervals

Do not apply; no estimates are made.

## P-values, if applicable

Do not apply.

## Missing data treatment

Does not apply.

## Stated limitations

Robinson flags that the argument of sections 5 and 7 is "extremely intricate" and that the adjusted-concavity
result is exact only under the small-elasticity-difference approximation, with a fuller treatment requiring more
complicated analysis (pp. 179, 194). She states that the conclusion that discrimination more often raises output
rests on "purely formal considerations" plus a conjecture about saturation in demand (pp. 201–202), and immediately
gives a counter-case in which a large, poor, more elastic market with convex demand makes output fall (p. 202). On
welfare she says it is impossible to say in general whether discrimination is desirable, and that a resource
misallocation cost must be weighed against any output gain, a subject she does not pursue (p. 206).

## Interpretation notes

- The price-ranking result is exact under the stated assumptions and follows directly from the p. 64 price rule
  applied market by market. It is the most robust and most testable claim in the chapters, because it needs only
  separable markets, a common marginal cost and profit maximization.
- The output results are conditional and mostly local. The straight-line neutrality result is exact; the general
  adjusted-concavity condition relies on an approximation that Robinson states. Her overall view that output is
  more likely to rise is a reasoned conjecture, and she gives the main counter-case herself.
- The claim that competition rules out discrimination (pp. 179–180) is stated for perfectly competitive markets. For
  imperfect competition she says only that gaps will usually be small. Testing it requires a measure of each seller's
  market power, which the book does not supply.
- The welfare discussion compares discrimination with simple monopoly, not with competition, and leaves the
  misallocation cost unquantified.
- None of these are analytical errors; they are boundaries of the results that Robinson herself marks. No published
  correction to these chapters was found or examined.

The modern rerun in this repository tests the price-ranking and market-power predictions on US college net prices
by family income, where market power is proxied by admission rates. It does not test the adjusted-concavity
condition, which would need demand curves, and its output comparison is cross-sectional rather than a comparison
of regimes.

## Unanswered questions

- How large are the price gaps the theory implies for plausible elasticities, and how do they compare with gaps
  observed in markets Robinson names (railway rates, surgeons' fees, export prices)?
- How should the theory be applied to sellers that do not maximize profit, such as the nonprofit institutions where
  discrimination by income is most visible today?
- Is the adjusted-concavity condition, or her saturation argument, borne out in any measured set of demand curves?
- How does the analysis change when sellers in an oligopoly coordinate discrimination, the "agreement between rival
  sellers" case she mentions on p. 180 but does not model?

## Items requiring expert verification

- The garbled footnote formulas on pp. 193–194 of the scanned text should be checked against a clean printed copy
  of the first edition.
- Whether the 1969 second edition altered chapters 15–16 or added a preface revising them was not checked.
- The relationship between Robinson's output conditions and the later necessary condition that welfare can rise only
  if output rises (Schmalensee 1981; Varian 1985) should be confirmed by a reader of those papers.
- The attribution of the straight-line result to Pigou (p. 192, note) and her criticisms of Pigou, Viner and Yntema
  (pp. 187, 201, 205) were taken from Robinson's text and not checked against the originals.
